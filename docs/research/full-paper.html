Cost-optimal load shifting for Moldovan households under a two-zone electricity tariff: a benchmark of greedy, simulated-annealing, genetic and exact schedulers

Stefania Tabarcea · grade 12 C · Orizont, Durlești · mentor: Azamat Jauysh
Category III — Information technology (algorithms, software applications) · MoldSEF 2026
Project “Energize the Future” (UN SDG 7) · https://energize-the-future.vercel.app · github.com/stefaniat2/energize-the-future
Abstract. Moldova’s two-zone household tariff makes night electricity about 22% cheaper, but the main fuse limits how much can run at once. We model the choice of start hours for flexible appliances as a constrained scheduling problem and compare a habit baseline, greedy placement, simulated annealing, a genetic algorithm and exact branch and bound on 393 seeded random households. Optimised schedules save 10.4% (5.5 kW fuse) and 5.6% (4.0 kW fuse) against habit (p < 0.001); for a reference household this is about 27 EUR a year. Exact search is instant at household size but proves optimality in only 8% of 40-appliance cases, where annealing is best. The method is implemented as a free browser tool in English, Romanian and Russian.

1. Introduction

About 655 million people still lack electricity (UN, 2024), and the electricity that is available is increasingly sold at prices that vary by hour. UN Sustainable Development Goal 7 asks for affordable and clean energy for all. In Moldova, households connected to low-voltage networks may opt for a tariff with two price zones. Premier Energy’s official page states 3.75 lei/kWh for 07:00–23:00 and 2.94 lei/kWh for 23:00–07:00, in force since 2 April 2026, against a flat price of 3.56 lei/kWh. The tariff is optional, requires a meter that records hourly use, and binds the household for 12 months.

A household can profit by running flexible appliances at night, but the main fuse (about 3.7 kW for 16 A, 5.75 kW for 25 A) limits the total power at every hour. Running everything at the cheapest hour would trip it. The question studied here is: can an algorithm choose start times that lower the bill without breaching the fuse, and which algorithm is best? The project began as an educational website about SDG 7 (learning pages, a strategy game, an appliance calculator) and was extended with this research module.

2. Problem formulation

There are N appliances. Appliance i has power pi (kW), a duration di (whole hours, one continuous run per day), an allowed set of start hours Si (a window of the 24-hour cycle) and a habitual start hour. A schedule assigns each appliance a start hour si ∈ Si; hours wrap around midnight. With hourly prices ch and a non-movable base load bh (refrigerator, lighting, cooking):

cost(s) = Σi Σk=0..di−1 pi · c(si+k) mod 24
Lh(s) = bh + Σi pi · [appliance i runs in hour h], overload(s) = Σh max(0, Lh − C)

where C is the fuse limit. The objective is to minimise cost(s) subject to overload(s) = 0. For solvers that explore infeasible schedules, the fitness is cost(s) + λ · overload(s) with λ = 10 · maxh ch, so one kWh above the fuse costs more than running anything at the highest price. For six appliances with unrestricted windows the search space has 246 ≈ 1.9 · 108 elements.

3. Methods

3.1 Habit baseline

Each appliance starts at its habitual hour; if that would exceed the fuse, it waits hour by hour until it fits. This is what a sensible household does without an optimiser. The raw overload of unchecked habits is reported separately. (An earlier version let habits ignore the fuse, which made the baseline unfair; see Section 6.)

3.2 Greedy placement

Appliances are sorted by energy pidi (largest first) and each is placed at the hour with the lowest incremental fitness, given those already placed. One pass, no backtracking.

3.3 Simulated annealing

Starting from the greedy schedule, 2400 iterations: pick one appliance and change its start (a one-hour shift or a jump to any allowed hour); always accept an improvement and accept a worse schedule with probability exp(−Δ/T). The temperature falls geometrically from 2 · max ch to 0.005 (Kirkpatrick et al., 1983).

3.4 Genetic algorithm

Population 40 (one member is the greedy schedule); tournament selection (size 3); uniform crossover (probability 0.9); mutation 12% per gene; the two best individuals survive each generation; stops after 2400 fitness evaluations (Holland, 1975).

3.5 Exact branch and bound

Appliances are placed in order of decreasing energy. A branch is pruned when the fuse is already exceeded (loads only grow) or when the cost so far plus the cheapest possible cost of the remaining appliances cannot beat the best schedule found (Land and Doig, 1960). A node limit of 3·106 bounds the run; when it ends before the limit, the optimum is proven.

4. Experimental design

Households. A seeded generator draws 5–7 appliances from a catalogue (washing machine, dishwasher, dryer, EV charger, water heater, pump) with realistic powers (0.8–3.7 kW) and durations (2–5 h). Each appliance is either unrestricted or limited to a plausible window; habitual hours are drawn from evening-heavy ranges; the base load has a morning and an evening bump. Regimes. Regime A uses a 5.5 kW fuse (typical 25 A connection) and 200 households; regime B uses 4.0 kW (tight) and 193 households (7 more had no feasible schedule and were excluded). Scalability. Sizes 6, 10, 16, 24 and 40 appliances, 100 households each, fuse scaled with size. Sensitivity. Night/day price ratios from 0.9 to 0.4 and three further tariff scenarios. Stability. 100 seeds on one hard 10-appliance household.

Metrics and statistics. Saving relative to habit; gap to the proven optimum (feasible schedules only); share of households where the optimum was found; share where the fuse was respected; run time (one laptop). Intervals are 95% confidence intervals of the mean; two methods are compared by a paired test on identical households (t statistic with a normal-approximation p-value, valid for n ≥ 30) with effect size dz. All randomness is seeded, so every number is reproducible with npm run benchmark.

H1 (supported). Annealing saves 10.4% (95% CI 9.9–10.9) in regime A and 5.6% (5.2–6.1) in regime B against habit (p < 0.001; dz = 2.7 and 2.1). Raw habitual start hours would exceed the fuse in 97% and 100% of households.

H2 (supported, with a caveat). In regime B the gap is 1.14% for greedy, 0.41% for annealing and 0.80% for the genetic algorithm (annealing vs greedy p < 0.001; genetic vs greedy p < 0.001). Both searches start from the greedy schedule and so can never end worse than it; part of the advantage is therefore built in. The fair reference is the proven optimum, which annealing finds in 103 of 193 households and the genetic algorithm in 63.

H3 (supported). At Premier Energy’s ratio 0.784 the optimum saves 10.5% (95% CI 9.8–11.2) against a ceiling of 21.6%. Across ratios 0.9 to 0.4 the saving rises from 4.7% to 34.0% and stays below the ceiling, at roughly half of it, because only part of the load can move without breaching windows and fuse. With the same code, the earlier 2025 media tariffs (4.32/3.31; 3.89/3.31; 4.89/3.90) give 11.4%, 7.1%, 9.8% — the saving depends on the tariff.

H4 (supported). Exact search finished its optimality proof for 100%, 99%, 49%, 27% and 8% of households with 6, 10, 16, 24 and 40 appliances. At 40 appliances it ended 3.94% above the best known schedule (the best of all methods, which favours the method that finds it most often) against 0.00% for annealing, in about 3 ms versus 584 ms. At household size exact search is already instant (0.4 ms), so heuristics matter for larger systems such as apartment blocks or schools.

Stability. On one hard 10-appliance household (proven optimum 186.69 lei) 100 seeds gave a mean of 188.11 ± 0.38 for annealing (28 runs found no schedule within the fuse) and 188.25 ± 0.05 for the genetic algorithm (4 failed runs). Neither method reliably finds the exact optimum on hard cases; they come close.

6. Design iterations and errors found

  1. Unfair baseline. In the first prototype the habit baseline ignored the fuse and broke it in 19 of 20 sample households, so its cost could not be compared with feasible schedules. It now waits until the fuse allows.
  2. Infeasible schedules in the gaps. The first analysis counted infeasible schedules in the cost gaps, which can flatter a method. Gaps now use only feasible schedules and the feasibility rate is reported separately.
  3. Stability test bug. The stability test used a fuse limit far too tight for 10 appliances; it was corrected by scaling the fuse with size.
  4. Random versus greedy start. With random starting schedules annealing respected the fuse in 95.9% of regime-B households (gap 0.83%) and 43 of 100 runs on the hard household failed. Starting from the greedy schedule improved this (final feasibility 97.9%, gap 0.41%), at the price of the built-in advantage discussed above.
  5. Out-of-date tariff. The first economic case used 2025 media figures (4.32/3.31 lei/kWh); reading the operator’s own page showed the 2026 prices (3.75/2.94), which lowered the estimated saving from about 33 to 27 EUR a year. The conclusions did not change; the money figures did.

The repair step is a mixed result: it lowers annealing’s gap (0.41% → 0.34%) and hard-case failures (28 → 18), yet the share of households with a feasible schedule falls (97.9% → 96.4%) because the repair takes budget away from the search, and it brings nothing to the genetic algorithm. It was therefore not adopted by default.

7. Economic analysis

A reference household moves 1,381 kWh a year: washing 208 × 1.0 kWh, dishwashing 260 × 1.0 kWh and water heating 365 × 2.5 kWh (assumptions, not measurements). Its shiftable load costs about 5,177 lei a year at the day price (the habit mostly runs in the day zone).

8. Software implementation

The algorithms are pure JavaScript functions (client/src/utils/optimizer.js) with seeded random numbers, so they run unchanged in the browser tool and in the benchmark script. The Smart Scheduler page lets a user choose a tariff, a fuse limit and appliances and compares the methods in milliseconds; the Research page renders the benchmark file and computes the hypothesis verdicts from it, so the page cannot disagree with the experiment. The surrounding website (React, Vite, Tailwind, Express, SQLite/Turso) offers learning pages, a budget strategy game and an appliance calculator in English, Romanian and Russian. Quality measures: 52 automated tests (including the exact solver checked against exhaustive search and a consistency test of the published benchmark), Lighthouse scores of 100 for accessibility, best practices and SEO and 95–98 for performance on the pages measured, text alternatives for every chart, and no personal data collected.

9. Limitations

  • Households are random but synthetic; no real Moldovan meter data was used.
  • Appliances are constant-power blocks that run once a day; real cycles vary.
  • The Premier Energy page does not state whether prices include VAT; FEE Nord’s two-zone prices for 2026 were not found.
  • The searches start from the greedy schedule, so their advantage over greedy is partly built in; annealing still fails on some hard tight-fuse cases.
  • Commercial prices are indicative; run times come from one laptop.

10. Conclusions

Under Premier Energy’s current two-zone tariff, load shifting is a modest but real and free saving (about 27 EUR per reference household a year) that must respect the fuse. For household-sized problems an exact solver is sufficient; metaheuristics, and annealing in particular, become necessary above roughly 16 appliances. The strongest next step is validation on real smart-meter data from a home or the school.
