Cost-optimal load shifting for Moldovan households under a two-zone electricity tariff: a benchmark of greedy, simulated-annealing, genetic and exact schedulers

Thematic category: III. Information technology (algorithms, software applications) · Author: Stefania Tabarcea · Grade: 12 C
Orizont, Durlești · mentor: Azamat Jauysh
Project “Energize the Future” (UN SDG 7) · https://energize-the-future.vercel.app · github.com/stefaniat2/energize-the-future

1. Aim and relevance of the research

Moldovan households can opt for a two-zone tariff in which night electricity (23:00–07:00) is cheaper than day electricity; Premier Energy’s official page gives 3.75 and 2.94 lei/kWh since 2 April 2026, a night discount of 22%. A household can lower its bill by moving flexible appliances (washing machine, dishwasher, dryer, electric water heater, EV charger) to the cheap zone, but the main fuse (about 3.7 kW for 16 A, 5.75 kW for 25 A) limits the total power at any hour. “Use the washer at night” therefore becomes a constrained scheduling problem: choose one start hour per appliance so that the daily cost is minimal and the fuse limit is never exceeded. For six unrestricted appliances there are 246 ≈ 1.9·108 combinations.

2. Hypotheses (written before the first full benchmark run)

  • H1. An optimised schedule costs less than the habitual one (paired difference, p < 0.05).
  • H2. With an equal budget of 2400 evaluations, simulated annealing and a genetic algorithm end closer to the optimum than greedy placement when the fuse limit is tight.
  • H3. The saving is bounded by (1 − night/day price) of the shiftable cost and grows roughly in line with the price gap.
  • H4. Exact branch-and-bound stops being usable as the number of appliances grows, while the metaheuristics keep returning feasible schedules in milliseconds.

3. Methods used

Every appliance has a power, a duration, an allowed start window and a habitual start hour. All methods minimise the same fitness (daily cost plus a large penalty per kWh above the fuse limit): (a) habit (reference: habitual hours, delayed only as far as the fuse requires); (b) greedy placement, largest energy first; (c) simulated annealing (Kirkpatrick et al., 1983) with geometric cooling; (d) a genetic algorithm (population 40, tournament selection, uniform crossover, mutation, elitism); (e) exact branch-and-bound (Land and Doig, 1960), used as the reference optimum. Annealing and the genetic algorithm start from the greedy schedule. Experiments use seeded random households (5–7 appliances): 200 households in regime A (5.5 kW fuse) and 193 in regime B (4.0 kW fuse; 7 infeasible households excluded), plus 100 households for each size of 6, 10, 16, 24 and 40 appliances. Gaps are measured against the proven optimum and use only schedules that respect the fuse; intervals are 95% confidence intervals; methods are compared with paired tests on identical households. All code, seeds and data are public and the benchmark is reproducible with npm run benchmark.

Mean saving versus habit and mean gap to the proven optimum, 200 (A) and 193 (B) households.

H1 supported. Annealing saves 10.4% (95% CI 9.9–10.9) in regime A and 5.6% in regime B versus habit (p < 0.001, dz = 2.7 and 2.1). Without waiting, the raw habitual start hours would exceed the fuse in 97% (A) and 100% (B) of households.

H2 supported, with a caveat. In regime B the mean gap is 1.14% for greedy, 0.41% for annealing and 0.80% for the genetic algorithm (annealing vs greedy p < 0.001; genetic vs greedy p < 0.001). Because both searches start from the greedy schedule, part of this advantage is built in; the comparison with the exact optimum is the fair one, and neither method reaches it on every household (optimum found in 103/193 and 63/193 cases).

H3 supported. At Premier Energy’s current ratio 0.784 the optimum saves 10.5% against a ceiling of 21.6%; across ratios 0.9 to 0.4 the saving rises from 4.7% to 34.0% and never exceeds the ceiling, because only part of the load can move.

H4 supported. Exact search finished its optimality proof for 100% of 6-appliance households but only 8% of 40-appliance households, where it ended 3.94% above the best known schedule (the best of all methods, so it favours the method that finds it most often) against 0.00% for annealing (about 3 ms versus 584 ms). At household size (6 appliances) exact search is already instant (0.4 ms), so metaheuristics matter only for larger systems such as apartment blocks or schools.

Design iteration. The first version started the searches from random schedules: in regime B annealing respected the fuse in 95.9% of households (gap 0.83%) and, on one hard 10-appliance household, 43 of 100 runs found no feasible schedule. Starting from the greedy schedule raised feasibility to 97.9% and lowered the gap to 0.41%, but 28 of 100 runs on the hard household still failed, so reliability on tight fuses remains a limitation.

5. Practical and economic value

A reference household moves 1,381 kWh a year (washing 208×1.0 kWh, dishwashing 260×1.0 kWh, water heating 365×2.5 kWh). At this tariff its shiftable load costs about 5,177 lei a year at the day price. The schedule saves 539 lei (≈ 27 EUR) a year; 291 lei with a tight fuse and at most 1,118 lei if everything moved to night (rate 20.18 lei/EUR, BNM, 5 Nov 2026). The software costs nothing and can be applied with delay-start timers already built into appliances (payback immediate); three smart plugs of about 15 EUR pay back in 1.7 years; a 658 EUR energy manager in 25 years; an installed system at about 1,000 EUR plus 120–240 EUR/year never, since its running cost exceeds the saving. Commercial prices are indicative (one retailer, one market report). No CO2 saving is claimed: moving consumption in time changes its price, not its amount.

6. Conclusions and limits

Under the reported Moldovan two-zone tariff, load shifting is a modest but real, free saving (about 27 EUR per household a year) that must respect the fuse limit. For household-sized problems an exact solver is sufficient; metaheuristics become necessary above roughly 16 appliances. Limits: synthetic households (no Moldovan meter data), tariff values that may change (VAT basis not stated), constant-power appliance blocks, timings measured on one laptop. Next step: validation on real smart-meter data.
