The 60-second summary

Energize the Future is a website about the United Nations' Goal 7: affordable and clean energy for everyone by 2030. About 655 million people still have no electricity, and the energy we do use is warming the planet. The website helps young people understand the problem (pages about SDG 7, six renewable energy sources, facts and tips), try solving it (a strategy game where you run a polluted city on a tight budget), and measure their own impact (a calculator that turns an appliance into money, CO₂ and "how many trees"). It speaks three languages and works on phones and computers.

It was created by stefaniat2 as a solo project, using an AI assistant (Claude Code) to write the code under the author's instructions, with the author reviewing, testing and publishing it. Everything is free and public.

Part 1

The project in plain words

What problem it addresses, who it is for, and what is special about it.

1.1 The problem: why "Goal 7"?

In 2015 the United Nations (UN) — an organisation of 193 countries founded in 1945 to keep peace and help countries work together — agreed on 17 "Sustainable Development Goals" to be reached by 2030. They cover things like ending poverty, clean water, education and protecting the climate. Goal 7 is called Affordable and Clean Energy. Its aim: everyone should have energy that is affordable, reliable, sustainable and modern.

Why does energy matter so much? Energy is not a luxury. It runs hospitals, schools, refrigerators that keep food and medicine safe, clean-water systems and phones. When it is too expensive or missing, families are held back. And when we make energy by burning coal, oil and gas (fossil fuels), we release gases that warm the planet and pollute the air.

Reading facts rarely changes habits. So the website turns the goal into things you do:

1.3 Who is it for?

Students (roughly 12–18) and their families, teachers and anyone curious. It works on phones, tablets and computers, needs no account and no installation, and is available in English, Romanian and Russian.

1.5 What makes it special

  • It is interactive, not a brochure. The game has a real budget: you cannot buy every good option, so you must choose — just like real governments.
  • It connects energy to your life. Enter your fridge's power and your electricity price and see your yearly cost, your CO₂, and how many trees would be needed to absorb it.
  • Three languages, everything translated — including the facts and game levels that come from the database.
  • Accessible and fast. Audited with Google's Lighthouse tool: 100 out of 100 for Accessibility, Best Practices and SEO on all pages.
  • Reliable and permanent. 29 automated tests, a permanent online database, free hosting, and all code and documents public.
  • Honest about AI. A dedicated page and document explain exactly what the AI did, what the author did, and what mistakes were found and fixed.
Part 2

How to use the website

A page-by-page tour: what each page is for, exactly what to click, and what you should see.

2.0 Opening the site

  • Address: energize-the-future.vercel.app — type it in any browser, or scan the QR code on the cover or the poster.
  • No account, no download, no password. It works in Chrome, Safari, Firefox and Edge, on phones and computers.
  • The first load may be slow. The data lives on a free server that "goes to sleep" after 15 minutes without visitors. The first request wakes it up, which can take up to about a minute. After that it is fast. Before presenting, open the site once and play one level to wake it up.

The menu and language button

At the top right are two buttons: the globe (language: English / Română / Русский — everything changes instantly, no reload) and the ☰ menu (opens a list of all ten pages; press Esc or click outside to close it). The logo on the left returns to the home page. On a phone the layout rearranges itself automatically.

2.1 Home

What it is for: a first impression and a map. Scroll down to see the SDG 7 explanation, the three objectives (affordable energy; reliable and sustainable energy; more renewable energy), four statistic cards, four energy-source cards, and six things you can do today.

What to say

"This is the home page. The goal of the project is to help young people understand clean energy and actually do something about it — there is a game, a calculator, facts and tips."

2.2 About SDG 7

Short sections explaining in simple words: what the UN is, what Goal 7 is, why affordable and clean energy matter, the problems of fossil fuels, energy poverty (when people cannot get or afford reliable energy), climate change, and why using less energy (energy efficiency) is the cheapest solution. It also has a timeline (from the discovery of the photovoltaic effect in 1839 to cheap solar and wind today) and the official targets 7.1, 7.2, 7.3, 7.a and 7.b, shown in simplified wording.

2.3 Renewable Energy

How to use: click any of the six sources (solar, wind, hydropower, geothermal, biomass, tidal). Scroll to the bottom for a comparison table showing how steady each source is, how its cost is changing, and where it works best.

In simple words

Renewable energy comes from sources that nature refills — sunshine, wind, flowing water, heat from underground, plants, and ocean tides. Fossil energy (coal, oil, gas) took millions of years to form and runs out.

2.4 Energy-Saving Tips

How to use: read a tip and press Mark as completed. The progress ring (for example "15% — 3 of 20 tips") updates and is remembered in your own browser — close the site and come back and it is still there. Reset progress starts over. Tips are grouped into Home, School, Transportation, Technology and Public Spaces.

2.5 Fun Facts

  • Category buttons (Solar, Wind, Climate, Energy Efficiency, Hydropower, Sustainable Technology) show only facts from that topic.
  • Random Fact jumps to any fact; Next Fact goes to the next one. "Fact 1 of 35" shows where you are.
  • The heart saves a favourite (see them under "My Favorites"); Share opens your phone's share menu or copies the fact.

These facts are not typed into the page — they are loaded from the database over the internet each time, in the language you chose.

2.6 Mini Game: Energy Hero

This is the star of the project. You are the new energy advisor of Greenville, a city that runs mostly on coal. Over 10 levels you make decisions that change the city.

The rules

  • Each level describes a situation (for example "The old streetlights consume huge amounts of electricity") and gives two options.
  • Each option has a cost. You start with a budget of 120 and earn +20 at every new level. An option you cannot afford is locked (shown with a padlock).
  • You have 25 seconds per decision. If the time runs out, the cheapest option is chosen for you.
  • Five city stats change with your choices: Clean Energy (higher is better), Pollution (lower is better), Affordability, Happiness and Sustainability Score.
  • After each decision a consequences panel shows exactly what changed (green = good, red = bad). Press Next Level to continue.

Scoring and the leaderboard

You earn points only for improvements, and sustainability is worth the most, so long-term thinking wins. For example, building the first level's solar farm gives 10×10 + 15×3 + 5×3 + 10×3 = 190 points. At the end you see your final score and any achievements you unlocked:

AchievementHow to get it
Clean StarterReach 50% or more clean energy
Pollution FighterBring pollution down to 20% or less
People's ChampionKeep happiness at 70% or more
Sustainability MasterFinish with a sustainability score of 80 or more
Energy HeroComplete all 10 levels

Press Submit to Leaderboard to save your score (it uses the name you typed, or "Anonymous Hero"). The top 10 scores are shown to everyone.

The clever part — the budget

Taking the best option on every level would cost 335, but you will only ever have 300. So even a perfect-looking game forces a sacrifice (usually at level 8, where the big power-plant replacement costs 50). Nobody can have everything — exactly like real governments. In testing, the best realistic score was about 2,090 points (the theoretical maximum is 2,436).

Careful when demonstrating

Scores cannot be deleted from the website. Do not submit practice scores before the event, and don't submit silly names in front of the jury. Playing without pressing "Submit" is always safe.

2.7 Energy Calculator

Add an appliance — a worked example

  1. Type a name, e.g. Refrigerator. Power: 150 (watts — printed on the appliance label). Hours per day: 24. Days per month: 30. Price per kWh: 0.15 (from an electricity bill). Pick a currency.
  2. Press Add Appliance. You will see: 3.6 kWh per day, 108 kWh per month, 16.20 per month, 194.40 per year, 48.6 kg of CO₂ per month.
  3. Under Your Totals the app adds everything up and says how many trees would be needed to absorb that CO₂ in a year (for this fridge, about 28).
  4. Use the pencil to edit and the bin to remove an appliance. Everything is saved in your browser.

Compare efficient alternatives

Further down, the page compares an old appliance with an efficient one using your electricity price: incandescent bulb vs LED, old vs A+++ fridge, old plasma vs modern LED TV, old vs inverter air conditioner. For the LED bulb it shows it saves about 85% of the power — roughly 93 kWh, 14 money units and 42 kg of CO₂ every year. One click adds the efficient version to your list.

In simple words

A watt (W) is how fast an appliance uses energy. A kilowatt-hour (kWh) is the amount of energy used: a 1,000-watt heater running for one hour uses 1 kWh. Your electricity bill charges you per kWh. The CO₂ number is only an estimate because it depends on how your country makes electricity — the page says so.

2.8 Community and 2.9 Contact

Community: enter a name, an e-mail, a category (renewable energy, efficiency, education, transportation, community projects, other) and a message of at least 10 characters. If something is wrong the page tells you exactly what to fix; if everything is right you see a "Thank you!" message and the idea is stored in the database. Contact works the same way for a message to the author (name, e-mail, subject, message). Neither form shows other people's messages — they are private.

2.10 AI & Credits

Explains which AI tool was used, what human oversight there was, where the facts come from, the open-source software and its licences, privacy (no accounts, no tracking), and the known limits. The page also links to the project's source code.

Part 3

How it was made

Told as a simple story, one step at a time. No programming knowledge needed — every new word is explained with an everyday comparison.

Don't worry — you will understand this

You do not need to understand code. You only need to understand five simple ideas. Everything in this part builds on them:

  1. Code is a recipe. A computer follows written instructions exactly.
  2. A website is like a restaurant. It has a dining room, a kitchen and a pantry.
  3. Each part uses a ready-made tool, like a kitchen uses an oven and a fridge.
  4. The restaurant needs a building that is always open. We rent three small free ones.
  5. Robots and people check it every time it changes, and an AI helper did most of the writing.

3.1 What does "making a website" even mean?

In one sentenceMaking a website means writing instructions, in a very strict language, that tell a computer what to show and what to do.

A computer cannot think. It cannot guess what you mean. It only does exactly what it is told, in the exact order. The instructions are called code. The special language they are written in is called a programming language.

Comparison: a recipe
A recipe= codeStep-by-step instructions
A cook who follows it exactly= the computerNever improvises, never gets tired
The language the recipe is written in= programming languageLike English or Romanian, but much stricter — one wrong letter and the cook is lost

Seeing it with your own eyes: three tiny examples

A web page is made of three kinds of instructions. Here they are in the simplest possible form. On the left is the real code. On the right is what the person sees.

1. HTML says WHAT is on the page.

The code<button> Play the game </button>
What you seePlay the game

"Put a button here that says Play the game." That's all. It's plain and ugly, but it exists.

2. CSS says HOW it looks.

The codebutton { background: green; color: white; border-radius: 20px; }
What you seePlay the game

"Make buttons green, with white writing and round corners."

3. JavaScript says WHAT HAPPENS.

The codewhen the button is clicked: add 1 to the number
What you seeClicked 0 times⬇ after one click ⬇Clicked 1 time

"When someone clicks, change the number." (This is simplified — real code looks a bit different — but the idea is exactly this.)

Comparison: a person

HTML is the skeleton (what is there). CSS is the clothes and make-up (how it looks). JavaScript is the muscles (what it does when you touch it). Our whole project is made of these three things. The code adds up to about 3,900 lines — roughly an 80-page booklet of instructions.

3.4 The kitchen — where the work happens

In one sentenceThe "backend" is a program on an always-on computer that keeps the information for everybody and answers requests from the website.

Why do we need a kitchen at all? Some things cannot happen on your phone. The leaderboard must show the same scores to everyone. Private messages must be saved somewhere safe. So we need a shared computer that is always switched on. A computer like that is called a server — "a computer that serves answers".

Node.js

What it isA tool that lets the JavaScript language run on a server.
Like…Having the kitchen staff speak the same language as the waiters. One author, one language, whole project.

Express

What it isA ready-made kitchen layout.
Like…A plan that says: "orders arrive here, and this cook handles soups, that cook handles desserts."
In our site"When someone asks for the facts, send them to the facts cook." Each job has its own small piece of code.

The waiter: how the dining room talks to the kitchen

The dining room cannot walk into the kitchen. It places an order and waits. The fixed menu of orders it is allowed to place is called an API. Here is a real conversation from this project:

The website (dining room)"Please give me all the fun facts, in English."
The server (kitchen)"Here are 35 facts." (and the list)

The reply is written as a tidy note called JSON — a neat way to write information so any program can read it:

{ "id": 1, "category": "solar", "text": "Sunlight reaches Earth with enough energy in one hour to power the entire world for a year." }

The orders on our menu, in plain words:

OrderWhat it means

The guards at the kitchen door

Before an order reaches the cook, it passes a few guards. Each guard has one simple job:

1🧐"Does this order make sense?"
Checks the information is sensible: a real e-mail shape, a name that isn't empty, a possible score. Nonsense is politely refused.
2⏱️"Too many orders!"
Stops spam. No single visitor can send more than 20 submissions a minute.
3🎫"Are you from our restaurant?"
The kitchen only accepts orders that come from this project's own website, not from strangers' websites.
One famous trick, and why it fails here

Some attackers type secret commands into a form, hoping the kitchen obeys them. In our project, everything a visitor types is treated as plain text only — like a note, never an instruction. The automated checks even try this trick on purpose to make sure it fails.

What it isThe language used to talk to a database.
ExampleSELECT * FROM facts means "show me everything on the facts sheet". It reads almost like English.

SQLite and Turso

What they areSQLite is the type of pantry: small, quick, and used in billions of phones. Turso is a company that rents you such a pantry online, for free for small projects.

Stocking the shelves ("seeding")

The facts and game levels are written in the kitchen's recipe book. When the kitchen opens, the cook checks the pantry. If a shelf is empty or the labels say old version, the cook restocks it. Each stock has a version number. If the author fixes a fact, a new version number makes the cook replace just the facts — while the leaderboard and messages are never touched.

Render's free plan lets the kitchen take a nap when nobody has visited for 15 minutes. The next visitor wakes it up, which can take up to about a minute. After that it is quick. That is why you open the site once before presenting.

Git and GitHub — the notebook of every change

3.7 Checking that everything works

Lighthouse — the report card

What it isA free checker built into Google's Chrome browser that gives a website marks out of 100.
Our marks Accessibility 100 — can everyone use it, including people with disabilities?
Best Practices 100 — does it follow modern, safe habits?
SEO 100 — can search engines understand it?
Performance 95–98 — does it load fast, even on a slow connection?
Honestly…At first it found real problems (for example some text was too pale to read). They were fixed. That is exactly what a report card is for.
What is "accessibility"?

Buildings have ramps for wheelchairs and signs in braille. A website needs the equivalent: clear colours, buttons with names that a screen reader can speak, a way to move around using only a keyboard, and a "skip to content" link. We built these in from the start.

The fact-check

What was doneEvery statistic on the site was compared with official sources (the UN, IRENA, Ember). One claim was wrong and was corrected: LED bulbs save at least 75% of the energy, not "up to 90%". The full list is in the file docs/FACT_CHECK.md.

3.8 The AI helper — how it was really built

In one sentenceAn AI assistant wrote the code, following written instructions from the author, who decided what to build, checked every result and published it.

An AI assistant is a program you can talk to in everyday language. Here it is called Claude Code. You tell it what you want. It writes the code, runs it and tests it on your computer. It is the only AI tool used in this project.

The builder lays the bricks. If you say "move that door", the builder does it. And sometimes the builder makes a mistake — which is why the manager must inspect.

How one step usually went

2🛠️The AI writes the code.
3🧪The robot checks run. The AI tests the page in a browser.
4👀The author looks at the result and says what is wrong.

The builder made mistakes — and they were found

This is the honest part, and it is important:

  • The game showed a "budget" but didn't actually use it. Fixed: options you can't afford are now locked.
  • The spinning wind turbine looked wrong. Fixed: redrawn.
  • A colour change made the footer text harder to read. Found by the report card (Lighthouse) and fixed.
  • The first public version asked the wrong address for its data, so pages showed errors. Found by looking at the live site and fixed.
  • The Romanian and Russian texts had grammar mistakes. Found in a second review and fixed.
The AI wrote the code under the author's instructions. The author decided what to build and checked the results. The AI made mistakes, and tests, an audit and reviews found and fixed them. Everything is explained openly on the website's "AI and Credits" page.
Honest limits

The Romanian and Russian reviews were done by an AI, not by a human proofreader, so a human read-through is still recommended. The CO₂ numbers are estimates. Statistics are rounded and change every year.

3.9 One click, step by step

3.10 Two clever parts, explained simply

The game: levels are data, not code

Each of the 10 levels is just a few lines on a sheet in the pantry: a story, two choices, and for each choice some numbers — for example "Invest in the wind farm: clean energy +20, pollution −12, affordability −5, sustainability +15, budget −40." To add a level, you add a line; you don't rewrite the game.

  • The budget. You start with 120 and earn 20 more per level. Taking every good choice would cost 335 but you only ever get 300 — so you must give something up. That's the clever part: real governments have the same problem.
  • The padlock. A choice is locked only if you can't afford it and the other choice is affordable, so you are never stuck.
  • The timer. You have 25 seconds. If the time runs out, the cheapest choice is made for you.
  • The score. Points only for improvements, and sustainability gives the most. Example: the first level's solar farm gives 100 + 45 + 15 + 30 = 190 points.
  • Fair play. The kitchen refuses impossible scores (the true maximum is 2,436), so nobody can cheat by sending a fake one.

The calculator: kitchen-table maths

Only multiplication and division. Example: a fridge that uses 150 watts, running 24 hours a day, for 30 days, at 0.15 per unit of electricity:

StepThe mathsResult
Energy per day150 ÷ 1000 × 243.6 kWh (a "kWh" is the unit your electricity bill uses)
Energy per month3.6 × 30108 kWh
Cost per month108 × 0.1516.20
Cost per year16.20 × 12194.40
CO₂ per month108 × 0.45 kg48.6 kg
Trees needed(48.6 × 12) ÷ 21 kg per treeabout 28 trees

The 0.45 and the 21 are simplified averages, so the site calls the CO₂ result an estimate. The maths is kept in its own small piece of code that the 29 checks test directly.

The project was created by stefaniat2 as a solo project. You are presenting it on their behalf. That is perfectly acceptable — but the jury will probably ask about how it was made, so you must never say or suggest that you programmed it or wrote it yourself. A simple, true opening sentence:

"This project, Energize the Future, was created by stefaniat2 as a solo project, using an AI assistant for the programming under their direction. I'm going to present it for them — what it is, how it works, and how it was made."
